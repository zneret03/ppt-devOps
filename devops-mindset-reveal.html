The oldest bug in software delivery

What the industry actually did about it

So We Shipped the Machine

A container image is that joke taken literally. Your code, its libraries, its operating system userland and its entrypoint, frozen into one file with one digest, that starts the same way wherever it lands.

WHAT TRAVELS

Inside the image

The application, its runtime, the system libraries it links against, the userland packages it shells out to, and the command that starts it. One file, one digest, one identity you can name in a deploy.

WHAT DOES NOT

Still shared, still yours

The kernel, the hardware, the clock, the network, the secrets and the data. A container is a process with a restricted view, not a machine. Everything shared is still shared.

WHAT CHANGED

Parity stopped being a promise

The same digest runs on a laptop, in CI, in staging and in production. “It works on my machine” became a checkable claim rather than an argument between two people.

WHY THE PIPELINE CARES

Built once, promoted forward. The artifact that passed your tests is byte for byte the one that reaches users, which is what makes the build-once rule enforceable instead of aspirational.

A real win, and a narrow one. An image guarantees the environment. It guarantees nothing whatsoever about the design inside it.

Correct the folklore early, because somebody in the room believes it. The complaint is decades older than Docker — people were saying it about Perl scripts and Tomcat WARs long before 2013. Containers are the answer to the sentence, not its origin. Saying so out loud buys you credibility and makes the joke land harder.

The middle card is the one worth labouring. Teams adopt containers and then act surprised when a kernel bug, a clock skew, a DNS change or a missing secret breaks the container that “works everywhere”. An image freezes the userland. It freezes nothing below it. “It works in my container” is the modern version of the same argument.

The dark band is the bridge into part 3 — say the phrase “built once, promoted forward” here and then say it again on the pipeline slide, deliberately. It is the same idea seen from the packaging end and the delivery end.

If someone asks why this matters for microservices: cheap process-level isolation is what made running twelve services affordable. Before containers, twelve services meant twelve VMs, and the maths did not work for most teams.

The problem it was built to solve

The Wall Between Dev and Ops

Development

Paid to change things.

  • Measured on features shipped
  • Wants the newest runtime
  • Tests on a laptop that works
  • Hands over a build and moves on

THE WALL

Operations

Paid to keep things still.

  • Measured on uptime
  • Wants the version it has patched
  • Carries the pager at 3am
  • Receives a build with no context

Both incentives are rational. The failure is structural: the people who can fix a production problem are not the people who caused it, and they meet for the first time during the outage.

The key line is the last one — say it slowly. Nobody in this picture is lazy or stupid. Two teams are each optimising exactly what they are measured on, and the sum of two rational local decisions is a bad global outcome.

If someone pushes back with "we don't have an ops team, we're all full-stack" — good, ask them who carries the pager and who decides the release date. The wall often survives a reorg by moving inside one team.

Origins

Ghent, 2009

THE TALK

Ten deploys a day

At Velocity 2009, John Allspaw and Paul Hammond described how Flickr's dev and ops teams shipped together, many times a day, without the release being an event.

THE MEETUP

The first DevOpsDays

Patrick Debois convened a two-day gathering in Ghent, Belgium, that October. The shortened hashtag people used to follow it became the name of the movement.

THE POINT

Not a tool purchase

Neither was about software you could buy. Both argued that the split between building and running a system was a choice an organisation had made, and could unmake.

Everything since then is elaboration: containers, pipelines, platform teams, SRE. The original claim is still the load-bearing one.

History earns you credibility cheaply. Most of the audience has never heard where the word came from, and knowing it changes how they argue about it at work — "DevOps engineer" as a job title looks odd once you know the movement was about removing a handoff, not creating a new specialist.

Worth saying out loud: the name is an accident of a hashtag. That usually gets a small laugh and it makes the point that the label was never the substance.

Definition

DevOps as an Operating Model

Not a job title and not a tool category. Four changes to how a team is wired, which everything else in this talk depends on.

Shared ownership

The team that builds a service also runs it. Werner Vogels put it at Amazon in 2006 as "you build it, you run it".

Paths as code

Anything repeatable and boring becomes a script in the repo: builds, environments, migrations, rollbacks.

Fast feedback

Production is the only honest source. Make its signals loud, fast and visible to the people who can act on them.

Blameless review

An incident is information about the system. Hunting for the person who typed it guarantees the next one is hidden.

This is the definition slide — the rest of the talk hangs off it. Map each of the four onto later sections: shared ownership → microservice boundaries, paths as code → the pipeline, fast feedback → DORA metrics and canaries, blameless review → the AI guardrails section.

The Vogels quote is real and from a 2006 ACM Queue interview. It is the single most useful sentence to hand a manager who asks what DevOps means.

Why these two words belong in one talk

Design and Delivery Are One Loop

A design is a hypothesis about load, failure and change. The delivery system is the only apparatus that ever tests it. Keep them apart and you get architecture nobody can ship, or shipping nobody can reason about.

Design sets the ceiling

You cannot deploy independently what you did not design to be independent. The architecture fixes your maximum release cadence before anybody writes a pipeline.

Delivery supplies the evidence

Every assumption — this is fast enough, this fails cleanly — is a guess until production disagrees. How long that loop takes is how long you hold a wrong guess.

Operations sends the bill

Timeouts, migrations, rotations, dashboards. None of it is ops overhead. Every line item was signed for by a design decision, usually months earlier.

This slide answers the question people are silently asking: why is one talk covering both? Because they are the same activity observed at two moments. Design is the claim; delivery is the experiment; operations is the result.

The strongest version of the argument: architecture determines deployability. If two components must ship together, no amount of pipeline engineering will let them ship apart — that was decided at design time. Equally, a beautiful decoupled design with a two-week manual release process never gets to prove it was decoupled.

Point at the dashed return arrow. Almost every team has the top row. The return leg is what separates a team that learns from a team that merely ships. Lead time is literally the length of this loop, which is why it is the metric that predicts the others.

Good line to use: "you cannot design your way out of a slow feedback loop, and you cannot deploy your way out of a bad boundary."

Part 01

System Design

The decisions you make before there is anything to measure.

Breath point. Say what this section is not: not a whiteboard-interview drill, not a catalogue of patterns. It is about the four questions that decide everything else, and the fact that every answer has a bill attached.

~15 sec

Four questions

The Job of System Design

Design is not drawing boxes. It is answering four questions in a way the team can defend six months later.

The questionWhat it decidesWhere it shows up
What must never be lost?Your source of truthDurability, backups, the write path
What must always answer?Your availability targetRedundancy and graceful degrading
How fast, at what load?Your latency budgetCaching, queues, indexes, p99
What can you afford?Your real constraintCost, and hours spent on call

The fourth question is the one that gets skipped, and the one that ends up deciding the other three.

Land the fourth row hard. Everyone can argue about consistency models. Almost nobody writes down the operational budget, and then the design that "won" on a whiteboard loses in month six because two people cannot keep it alive.

A concrete prompt for the room: "if your answer to question two is four nines, that is 52 minutes of downtime a year including deploys and cloud provider incidents. Who is awake to defend it?"

~2 min

A menu, not a ladder

Architectures, and What Each Solves

None of these is more advanced than the one above it. Each is an answer to a specific question, and answering a question nobody asked is the most expensive thing an architect can do.

The shapeThe problem it solvesWhat it charges you
Layered monolithOne team shipping one product, quicklyEverything scales and fails together
Modular monolithClear seams without a network between themDiscipline nothing enforces for you
MicroservicesTeams releasing independently of peersLatency, tracing, and N of everything
Event-drivenProducers that need not know consumersOrdering, replays, eventual consistency
Serverless functionsSpiky load you refuse to pay for when idleCold starts, limits, awkward local runs

Almost every real system is two or three of these at once. The mistake is adopting one as an identity rather than as an answer.

Say the framing before the rows: this is a menu, not a maturity ladder. Teams get into trouble by reading it top to bottom as a progression and assuming they are behind. A layered monolith run by six people shipping daily is not a less evolved microservice estate; it is the right answer to a different question.

Row two is the underrated one. A modular monolith gives you most of the boundary discipline of services with none of the network, and it is the right destination for far more teams than admit it. Its honest cost is in the right-hand column: nothing stops a developer reaching across a seam at 5pm on a Friday except a code review.

Row four is the one people adopt by accident. Nobody decides to go event-driven; they add one queue, then another, and two years later they own ordering guarantees, replay semantics and a consistency model they never wrote down.

Row five: the point of serverless is the cost model, not the code model. If your load is flat, you are paying a complexity premium for an elasticity you do not use.

Good closing prompt: "which question is your architecture the answer to, and does anyone still ask it?"

~2 min · this is the setup for the live demo, so end on row five

The tactical vocabulary

Patterns, and the Failure Each Prevents

Each of these exists because a specific, boring disaster happened to somebody first. Learn them as failures avoided, not as diagram furniture.

PREVENTS

Circuit breaker

A dependency’s outage becoming yours. After repeated failures, refuse instantly instead of holding a thread for the full timeout.

PREVENTS

Idempotency key

A retry charging the card twice. The caller sends a key, the server remembers the answer it already gave and returns it again.

PREVENTS

Transactional outbox

An order saved while its event is never sent. Write the row and the message in one transaction; a relay publishes it afterwards.

PREVENTS

Saga

A distributed transaction you cannot roll back. Each step gets a compensating step, and the business decides what undo means.

PREVENTS

Bulkhead

One slow tenant eating every thread. Separate pools per caller or per route, so the noisy neighbour drowns on its own.

PREVENTS

Strangler fig

A big-bang rewrite that never lands. Route one path at a time to the new system until nothing reaches the old one, then delete it.

Four of these are switches on the next slide. You are about to watch two of them turn a dependency failure into either a shrug or an outage.

Every pattern here is a remembered disaster. Teach them that way and they stick; teach them as boxes on a diagram and they get cargo-culted.

The pairing that matters most: circuit breaker and retry are not independent. A retry without a breaker is the single most reliable way to convert a partial degradation into a full outage, because you double the load on the thing that is already failing. You demonstrate exactly this on the next slide, so promise it here.

Outbox is the one most teams meet too late. The dual-write problem — save to the database, then publish to the broker — has no safe ordering: crash between the two and you have either a lost event or a phantom one. It is invisible until it is a reconciliation project.

Saga: say clearly that a compensating action is not a rollback. A refund is not the inverse of a charge; the money moved, the customer noticed, and an email went out. That is a product decision wearing an architecture costume.

Strangler fig is named after the plant that grows around a tree until the tree is gone. Its real value is that it is reversible at every single step, which is the opposite of a rewrite.

Design decisions

Break something

Model costs: edge 20ms, gateway 15ms, service hop 12ms, stock read 30ms (2ms cached), capture 140ms, write 18ms, timeout 800ms.

Demo script — do it in this exact order, it builds.

1. Send one request with everything off. Narrate the packet. Point out that the 140ms capture is over half the total.

2. Turn on Cache the stock read. Send again. Small win — and say the cost out loud: you just accepted stale stock, which means overselling.

3. Turn on Payments provider is down, everything else off. Send. The 800ms timeout is visible and slow, which is the point. Say: every thread sitting here is a thread not serving healthy traffic.

4. Now add Retry once. Send. It is twice as slow and twice as much load on something already dying. This is the row-five payoff from the previous slide — let it land.

5. Turn the retry off, turn on Circuit breaker. 50ms. Still a failure, but a cheap one.

6. Finally turn on Take payment asynchronously. The order succeeds even though payments is down. Ask the room what you just traded away — answer: the user no longer finds out at checkout, and you now own a reconciliation problem.

Then hit Run 100 and point at the p50/p99 gap: averages hide the tail, and your users live in the tail.

This is the practical payoff of the loop slide. It converts "design and delivery are connected" from a slogan into five concrete rows a team can check their own decisions against.

Row three is the one that bites hardest in practice. A shared database is the most common reason a microservice migration stalls: the services are separate in the diagram and welded together in the schema, so every release still needs coordination. Expand-and-contract is the way out, and it is a delivery technique that only works if the design left room for it.

Useful challenge for the room: take your last architecture decision and write its right-hand column on a whiteboard tomorrow. If the column is empty, you moved the cost somewhere you cannot see it.

Part 02

Microservices

An old idea about small programs, pushed across a network.

Frame the section as archaeology, not advocacy. You are going to explain where the idea came from, what problem it actually solves, and what it charges you. You are not going to tell anyone to adopt it.

~15 sec

Before you split anything

The Monolith You Start With

A single deployable is not technical debt. For most teams it is the correct answer for longer than they think.

One build. One place to look when something breaks. Function calls that cannot time out, retry or arrive twice. Transactions that are actually transactions. Refactoring across a boundary costs a rename, not a migration and a deprecation window.

Split when the organisation hurts, not when the diagram looks untidy.

SIGNALS IT IS TIME

  • One team's release blocks four others
  • A single hot path forces you to scale all of it
  • Any change means a full regression run
  • One bad code path takes the whole process down
  • Two parts need different uptime promises

Defend the monolith properly or the rest of the section sounds like a sales pitch. Say plainly that most teams in the room should not split, and that "modular monolith" is a perfectly respectable destination, not a waypoint.

Every signal in the right-hand box is organisational or operational. None of them is "the code is messy" — splitting a mess gives you a distributed mess with worse debugging.

Observed at every conference, including this one

"Every architecture decision ever made."

The question is never whether the new thing is better. It is what the old thing needed that you are about to stop giving it.

Self-deprecating works best here — you are giving the conference talk in question. Say so.

Lineage

Where Microservices Came From

1970s

Unix pipes

Small programs that each do one thing, composed into something larger.

2000s

SOA and the bus

Services were the right unit. The clever bus in the middle became the bottleneck.

2002

The API mandate

Amazon teams could reach each other's data only through service interfaces.

2009

Cloud gets cheap

Netflix begins moving to AWS. The same year, DevOpsDays first meets in Ghent.

2011 to 2012

The name sticks

Architects meeting near Venice use "microservice"; a year later the group settles on it.

2013 to 2015

Tooling catches up

Containers and orchestration make many deployables something a small team can run.

The name arrived in 2011 and 2012. The practice was already running in production at Amazon and Netflix.

The punchline is the last line: the practice predates the word by a decade. That matters because it means nobody invented microservices as a good idea — several teams arrived at the same shape independently, under pressure, for organisational reasons.

Details you can offer if asked: the Venice workshop was May 2011; the same group settled on the plural name in May 2012; James Lewis presented the case study at 33rd Degree in Kraków; Netflix called their version "fine-grained SOA" before adopting the term. Lewis and Fowler's article followed in 2014.

The 2002 Amazon mandate is the most interesting card — it was a management instruction about how teams communicate, and the architecture fell out of it. That is Conway's Law being used on purpose, two slides ahead.

Lineage, again

Where Containers Came From

Docker invented almost none of the isolation it is famous for. Every primitive was already sitting in a kernel somewhere.

YearWhat arrivedWhat it gave us
1979chroot, in Unix Version 7A process with a different idea of where “/” is
2000FreeBSD jailsIsolation promoted to a security boundary
2004Solaris ZonesMulti-tenancy on one kernel, in production
2006 to 2008cgroups and namespacesLimits, and processes that cannot see out
2008LXCThe kernel pieces assembled into containers
2013DockerThe image, the Dockerfile, the registry
2015OCI and KubernetesA standard format, and something to schedule it

Docker’s contribution was packaging and distribution, not isolation: a layered image format, a build file anyone could read, and a place to push it. That is why it won in eighteen months.

This is the same move as the microservices lineage slide, and the same punchline: the practice predates the famous name. Use it to make a point about engineering credit generally — the winning technology is usually not the one with the best primitives, it is the one with the best distribution story.

Details you can offer if pressed: cgroups began at Google in 2006 as “process containers”, were renamed in 2007 to avoid the word, and landed in Linux 2.6.24 in 2008. Docker itself ran on LXC for its first year before replacing it with libcontainer in 2014. The Open Container Initiative in 2015 turned the image and runtime formats into a standard, which is why docker build output runs under containerd, CRI-O or Podman today.

The line to land: the Dockerfile is the reason. Fifteen readable lines that a developer could write on a Tuesday, producing an artifact an operator could run on a Wednesday. That is a social invention as much as a technical one, and it is exactly the handover this whole talk is about.

Cuttable if you are behind schedule — the previous slide carries the argument on its own.

"Make each program do one thing well."

Doug McIlroy, Bell Labs, 1978

~45 sec · good place to take a breath and check the clock

Melvin Conway, 1967

Conway's Law

An organisation that designs a system produces a design whose structure copies its own communication structure. You do not get to opt out of this.

Four teams, one release train

Four modules in one deployable. Coordination happens in meetings, and the slowest team sets the release date.

Four teams, four deployables

Coordination happens in contracts instead. Four pipelines, four on-call rotations, four sets of dashboards.

The manoeuvre: if you want the second architecture, change the team boundaries first. The code follows the org chart, not the other way round.

This is the most actionable slide in part 2. Conway's Law is usually quoted as a curse; the useful version is the inverse manoeuvre — deliberately reshape teams to get the architecture you want. Amazon's 2002 mandate was exactly that, and two-pizza teams were the mechanism.

Practical test for the room: count how many teams have to be in a room to ship your most common change. That number is your real architecture, whatever the diagram says.

~2 min

The hard part

Drawing Service Boundaries

A BOUNDARY THAT WILL HURT

Split by technical layer

A UI service, an API service, a database service. Every feature crosses all three, so every feature needs three deploys, three reviews and three teams in the same room.

You kept all the coupling and added a network.

A BOUNDARY THAT WILL HOLD

Split by business capability

Ordering, Payments, Inventory. A feature usually lives inside one. Each owns its data, its schema and its uptime promise, and publishes only what the others need.

The boundary uses words the business already says.

THE TEST

Name the change your users ask for most often. Count the services it touches. If the answer is more than one, the boundary is in the wrong place.

The left card is the most common real-world failure. It happens because technical layers are the seams people can already see in the codebase, and because splitting by layer maps neatly onto existing specialist teams — which is Conway's Law producing exactly the wrong answer.

The test in the dark band is the thing to write down. It is cheap, it takes five minutes, and it is falsifiable. Offer it as the one takeaway if they remember nothing else from this section.

If someone asks about bounded contexts and DDD: yes, this is that, minus the vocabulary. Point them at Sam Newman's Building Microservices for the long version.

Two ideas carry this slide. First: build once. ~3 min

Three words, often used as one

Integration, Delivery, Deployment

CI

Continuous integration

Everyone merges into trunk several times a day, and every merge builds and runs the tests. The point is to meet a conflict within minutes of creating it, while it is still one person's problem.

CD

Continuous delivery

Every green build produces an artifact that could go live right now. Releasing becomes a decision somebody makes in a second, rather than a project somebody runs for a fortnight.

CD

Continuous deployment

Every green build goes, with nobody in the path. You reach this only once the tests, the canary and the rollback are things you would bet the weekend on.

Aim for delivery first. Deployment is what you earn afterwards, and plenty of good teams stop at delivery on purpose.

Most teams say "CI/CD" and mean "we have a Jenkins job". The distinction matters because it tells you what to fix next: if merges are painful, that is a CI problem; if the artifact is ready but sits for three weeks, that is a delivery problem; if you are afraid to automate the last step, that is a testing-and-rollback problem.

Be explicit that continuous deployment is not the goal for everyone. Regulated environments, on-premise shipping and safety-critical systems stop at delivery on purpose and are not doing it wrong.

That is the honest argument for the canary: there is a class of defect no test suite can reach, so you need a release strategy that limits how many people meet it. Say that here, then run the demo on the next slide and let the injected runtime bug prove it.

Do not let this land as "testing is pointless" — the pipeline demo shows a unit test catching a defect in eight minutes for almost nothing. Both things are true.

How the change reaches users

Four Ways to Release

Deploying and releasing are different acts. These four decide how many users meet a bad change before you notice it.

Rolling

Replace instances a few at a time until the fleet is on the new version.

Costs: both versions serve real traffic at once, so the contract must hold both ways.

Blue-green

Two complete environments, one switch, and a switch back if it goes wrong.

Costs: you pay for two of everything, and shared state must agree with both.

Canary

A small slice of traffic first, held against the same service objectives.

Costs: useless without metrics good enough to compare two populations.

Feature flag

Deploy the code dark, then turn it on for whoever you choose, whenever.

Costs: every flag is a branch in production, and they rot if nobody removes them.

All four assume one thing: you can go back in a single step, and you have done it recently enough to believe it.

Open with the distinction: deploy is putting code on a machine, release is letting users reach it. Feature flags exist entirely to separate those two, and once a team understands that, Friday-deploy anxiety mostly evaporates.

The closing line is the real content. A rollback you have never rehearsed is a rollback you do not have, in the same way that an untested backup is not a backup.

If you measure one thing, measure these

Measuring Delivery, Not Activity

The signalWhat it actually tells you
Deployment frequencyHow small your batches really are
Lead time for changesHow long an idea waits inside your system
Change failure rateHow often a release makes things worse
Time to restore serviceHow fast you can undo a mistake
Rework rateHow much work exists only to fix the last release

Not one of these measures how busy anybody is. That is the entire point, and it is why they survive contact with management.

The four keys come from the DORA research programme; rework rate was added in the later reports.

The four keys are two speed metrics and two stability metrics, and they move together. That was the counter-intuitive finding when the research first landed: elite performers deploy far more often and fail less. Speed and safety are not a trade-off at the system level, which is why "go slower to be safe" is usually the wrong prescription.

Where this goes wrong: teams turn deployment frequency into a target and start deploying empty commits. Metrics that become targets stop measuring. Use them as a thermometer, never as a quota.

If asked about the 2025 report: DORA reframed around AI-assisted development, added rework rate, and replaced the four performance tiers with seven team profiles. The four keys are still the backbone.

The pipeline is the only path to production.

No console, no manual copy, no exception for the urgent fix at eleven at night. The moment a second path exists, every guarantee the first one gives you becomes a guess.

Pause. This is the sentence to leave the room with from part 3.

The argument in one move: the value of a gate is not that it exists, it is that nothing can go around it. One emergency SSH deploy invalidates your entire audit trail, your reproducibility claim and your rollback story simultaneously.

Expect pushback: "what about a real emergency?" The answer is that the emergency path is the same pipeline with a shorter soak, pre-approved and rehearsed — not a different mechanism invented at 2am by a tired person.

Three different things, one word

From Suggestion to Action

Most disappointment with AI in operations comes from buying one row and expecting another. They fail in different ways, so they need different controls.

KindWhere it sitsWhat it doesWhat you must own
CopilotIn the editorSuggests code and testsReviewing every line
AIOpsIn the telemetryGroups alerts, flags driftThe quality of signals
AgentIn the systemInvestigates, then actsIts blast radius
WORTH REMEMBERING

The 2025 DORA research found AI adoption raising throughput and instability at the same time. It amplifies the system you already have, in whichever direction it is already pointing.

The distinction that matters is suggests / correlates / acts. A copilot hands you a draft and waits. AIOps tells you which alerts belong together. An agent forms a hypothesis, tests it against live telemetry and then does something. Only the third one can take your site down.

On the DORA finding: the useful framing is "amplifier, not fixer". Strong teams got faster; struggling teams got faster at producing rework. It also surfaced that the downstream bottleneck moves to review, testing and QA — which is the argument for the guardrails on the next slide.

If asked what to adopt first: the copilot row, because the blast radius is a code review. Earn the agent row.

~3 min

Before you let it touch production

Guardrails Before Autonomy

The same controls you would put on a new engineer with production access — written down and enforced by the platform instead of by trust.

A bounded blast radius

One namespace, one budget, one list of permitted actions. Restart a pod, yes. Touch the primary database, never.

The same one path

An agent's change is a pull request that runs the tests and the canary, like anybody else's. No side door, however clever the agent is.

Evidence, not claims

A proposed cause arrives with the traces, logs and diffs behind it, so a tired human can check the reasoning in a minute.

A one-step undo

Every automated action has a reversal rehearsed as often as the action, and an audit trail naming what ran and why.

None of this is AI work. It is the platform work you needed anyway, which is why strong teams get more out of AI than weak ones.

The closing line is the thesis of the whole talk, so deliver it deliberately. Every guardrail on this slide is something you should already have: least privilege, one path to production, observability you can reason from, and a tested rollback. AI did not create these requirements, it made skipping them expensive.

Card two is the one people argue about, because agents are fastest when you let them act directly. Hold the line: the pipeline is the only path to production applies to non-human contributors too.

Five things, none of them a purchase

Where to Start on Monday

01Time the last twenty changes from merge to production. Measure before you fix anything.
02Make the build reproducible: one command, one artifact, no special machine.
03Add one gate to the pipeline that is allowed to say no, and then let it.
04Rehearse a rollback on your newest service. Time that too.
05Delete one manual step this week. Then another next week.

Notice that none of these require a budget, a migration, or permission from an architecture board.

This is the slide that decides whether the talk changes anything on Monday. Every item is doable by one engineer inside a normal week without asking anyone.

Item one is the most important and the one people skip. Without a baseline, every later improvement is an anecdote. Item four is the one that produces the biggest surprise — most teams discover their rollback does not work.
